Nvidia Brings Huang Back and Markets Roar, the Riemann Proof Passes Its Formal Check, and OpenAI's Chief Scientist Asks for Shared Limits

Nvidia's board reinstates its founder, the Lean and Mathlib communities certify the Claude-assisted Riemann Hypothesis proof, Jakub Pachocki proposes industry limits tied to perplexity, a startup says AI agents designed a new kind of CPU, Wells Fargo's agent-liability terms take effect, and FreeVideo runs a full video model on an 8 GB graphics card.

Viral / open-source spotlight

FreeVideo: a full video-generation model on an 8 GB graphics card

FreeVideo (github.com/FlashML-org/FreeVideo) is an open-source tool that generates video on an ordinary home computer. Its stated minimum is a graphics card with 8 GB of memory and 16 GB of system RAM, hardware that normally cannot hold a modern video model at all. It runs a model the project calls MiniMax H3, installs through a launcher on Windows, macOS or Linux, and plugs into ComfyUI, the popular node-based interface for image and video generation. One GitHub trend tracker listed it on November 22 at about 4,100 stars with roughly 400 added that day, among the fastest-growing AI repositories by share; another listing shows far fewer, so treat the count as approximate. Reports say the licence is Apache 2.0. The trick is memory juggling. The model is a stack of 50 large blocks, and only some fit on the card at once. Before each request a planner measures the machine and decides which blocks stay in graphics memory, which wait in ordinary RAM and which are streamed from disk when needed. It also picks compressed 8-bit weights suited to the card. It is like cooking a banquet in a small kitchen: you cannot lay out every ingredient, so you keep on the counter only what the current step needs and fetch the rest from the pantry. Why it is taking off: Video generation has been the one kind of AI that hobbyists could not run at home; it has meant paying per clip to a cloud service and accepting its content rules and queues. FreeVideo trades time for money: on a minimum machine it works, slowly and with heavy disk use, but it is private and has no per-clip fee. It fits the local-first pattern of recent spotlights such as Linger (Day 238) and Orkas (Day 234). The caveats: the 8 GB figure is the project's own claim, speed on small cards is poor by users' accounts, we could not verify the underlying model or its licence terms for commercial use, and cheap private video generation also makes convincing fake footage cheaper. Also trending: Claude-BugHunter, a bundle of security-testing skills for Claude Code, and autoclip, which cuts long videos into short clips.

Part 1Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang and Markets Rebound

On Monday, November 23, Nvidia's board voted unanimously to bring Jensen Huang back as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. Colette Kress, interim chief executive since Huang resigned on November 12 (Day 230), returns to her job as chief financial officer, and Harvey Jones stays as board chair. It is a sharp reversal: on November 17 Jones had said the board did not intend to reinstate him. According to reports of the company's filing, the board said it had received input from senior levels of the US government and was confident the return poses no national security risk. Huang had stepped down after press reports about a personal relationship that US officials reportedly feared was tied to foreign intelligence. A committee of independent directors is still investigating what the board calls a foreign intelligence effort targeting Huang, and a new National Security and Export Control Committee will oversee trade compliance. President Trump said he had pressed for the return; Senator Warren said Nvidia still owes Congress answers, and three House committees still plan a closed-door interview with Huang on December 1. Nvidia shares rose about 31% to roughly $165, adding about $950 billion in value, the stock's best day since May 2003. The Nasdaq rose 6.5% and the S&P 500 4.7%, after a week in which the Nasdaq had fallen about 10% (Day 240). The concept, explained simply: Key-person risk is the danger that a company's value depends on one individual. Think of a restaurant famous for its chef: the building, the staff and the recipes are all still there if the chef leaves, but customers may not be. Monday's jump is that risk running backwards, a market paying nearly a trillion dollars for one person's return. The board's tool for handling the awkward part is an independent committee: a group of directors with no ties to management who investigate and report back, like asking a referee from another league to review a disputed call. Keeping that committee running while the person under review returns to work is unusual, and it is the detail to watch. Why it matters: Nvidia supplies most of the chips the AI industry runs on, so its leadership is everyone's supply-chain question. The episode also shows how closely government and the largest AI firms are now tied: a board cited government input in a decision about its own chief executive. A one-day rebound does not answer the questions from last week about borrowed money and circular financing; it only removes one source of doubt. The SEC inquiry (Day 239) and the congressional interest continue.

Part 2Lean and Mathlib Certify the Riemann Hypothesis Proof

Yesterday's open question was whether anyone independent would confirm that the formal statement in Levent Alpöge's archive really is the Riemann Hypothesis. That has now happened. The Lean and Mathlib communities published a joint statement, endorsed by the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative, saying they have full confidence in the result. They checked two things. First, that the theorem in Alpöge's library is exactly the definition called RiemannHypothesis in Mathlib, the community's shared library, which Lean experts and number theorists had vetted; two separate comparison tools reported an exact match. Second, that Lean's official kernel and several other independent proof checkers accept the exported proof. One report says a separate sandboxed rebuild reproduced all of the roughly 61.6 gigabytes of the published export. Some loose ends remain: one source says Andrew Sutherland's full end-to-end run at MIT was still listed as in progress, and outlets disagree on whether the result also covers the generalised version of the hypothesis. No expert has yet published a human-readable account of why the proof works.

The concept, explained simply: A proof checker earns trust by keeping the part you must trust very small. Lean's kernel is a short program that knows only the basic rules of logic. However huge the proof and whoever wrote it, human or AI, every step is reduced to those rules and replayed through the kernel. It is like a bank that does not care how clever your accountant is; it only checks that each line adds up. Running several different checkers, written by different people, guards against a bug in any one of them. The other half of trust is the statement: the comparison tools confirm the theorem proved is letter-for-letter the one mathematicians agreed on, so nobody has proved a look-alike.

Why it matters: This closes the gap we flagged on Day 240 between 'the file compiles' and 'it proves the right thing'. It does not close the second gap, understanding: a proof this large that nobody can read teaches little until people digest it. For everyday work the method is the lesson. Trust in AI output came here not from the model or its maker but from a small, independent checker and an agreed specification. Where you can build that pair, such as tests written before the code or a schema the output must satisfy, you can accept much larger pieces of AI work.

Part 3OpenAI's Chief Scientist Proposes Shared Limits on AI

On November 22 Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, published an essay titled 'Perplexity and Intelligence'. He proposes an industry-wide standard that sets an initial limit on AI development and spells out the milestones developers must reach before the limit can be raised, with progress gated on how well humans can understand and monitor AI behaviour. Labs would agree on shared test methods and check one another's safety claims. He warns that AI could start speeding up training advances as early as 2027, faster than safety work can follow. Sam Altman endorsed the idea, writing that the industry should work toward agreement on common limits. The essay names no number, no auditor and no regulator, and Pachocki concedes the weaknesses: labs measure on different data, and some capability gains never show up in the metric. The context is OpenAI's own report of November 18, which estimated that its agents completed 46% of two-to-four-hour research tasks without help in October, up from 23% in March, while warning that researchers also changed which tasks they hand over. The concept, explained simply: Perplexity measures how surprised a language model is by real text. A model predicts the next word; if it is usually nearly right, its perplexity is low. Picture a guessing game: a perplexity of 10 means the model is, on average, as unsure as someone choosing between ten equally likely words, and a perplexity of 3 means only three. It is cheap to measure and hard to argue with, which is why it appeals as a shared speedometer. The weakness is that a speedometer does not tell you where the car can go. Tools, planning and longer reasoning can make a system far more capable without changing how well it predicts the next word.

Why it matters: A call for limits from the chief scientist of a leading lab, backed by its chief executive, is new. It moves the idea of capability thresholds (the Call for Control, Day 239) from outside critics to inside the industry. The test is whether a number, an independent measurer and a consequence follow; a limit that each lab measures on its own private data is a promise, not a standard. Any metric that becomes a target also tends to get gamed. Buyers should expect vendors' safety claims to start citing shared thresholds and should ask who measured them.

Part 4A Startup Says AI Agents Designed a New Kind of CPU

Architect Labs says its AI agents designed Frostbite, a general-purpose processor with its own instruction set, from start to finish. Instead of stepping through instructions as x86 and Arm chips do, Frostbite is built around interaction nets, a model in which a program is a graph and computing means rewriting connected pairs of nodes. The company claims 4.59 times the performance per watt of a conventional small in-order core, averaged over 25 workloads such as matrix multiplication, JSON parsing and decompression, and about 8.8 times the performance per unit of chip area. The detail worth reading: the gain was 6.02 times on the 10 workloads the agents saw while designing and 3.83 times on the 15 held back until the end. All of it comes from simulation; no chip has been made. The comparison core is not named, existing software will not run without being rewritten for the new model, and nobody outside the company has reproduced the numbers.

The concept, explained simply: A held-out set is a batch of tests the designer is not allowed to see until the work is finished, like an exam paper kept sealed while you revise from past papers. Scores on the past papers show how well you learned those questions; the sealed paper shows whether you learned the subject. A drop from 6.02 to 3.83 is the usual sign of some tuning to the practice set, though 3.83 would still be a large gain. Pre-silicon means the figures come from a software model of the chip. Real silicon adds heat, wiring delay and manufacturing limits, which usually shrink such numbers.

Why it matters: After the Riemann proof, this is a second claim in a week that AI agents did open-ended technical design rather than assist with it. It is far less verified: one is certified by an independent checker, the other rests on the company's own simulator and an unnamed baseline. Use the same questions for any 'AI designed X' headline: was there a held-out test, who chose the baseline, and has anyone else reproduced it? It also connects to section 3, since AI designing better chips for AI is one of the feedback loops Pachocki worries about.

Part 5Wells Fargo's AI-Agent Terms Put the Risk on Customers

Wells Fargo's updated Online Access Agreement takes effect today, November 24, with a new section on third-party AI agents. According to press coverage (we could not open the agreement itself), a customer who lets an agent into their account is responsible for what it does, including unintended actions, mistakes in interpreting instructions, cases where the agent goes beyond the authority it was given, and actions caused by manipulated inputs, which would cover prompt injection. Customers who cut off an agent must keep evidence that they did so and show it to the bank on request. The bank may require agents to be registered and may block them, and the terms apply subject to applicable law. Other large banks have gone the other way: Citi says it does not allow third-party agents to log in or transact at all, and several others restrict them. The concept, explained simply: This is about who pays when a delegate gets it wrong. US rules on electronic payments protect you when a stranger takes money from your account, but if you hand someone your card and PIN, what they do generally counts as authorised by you until you tell the bank otherwise. Wells Fargo is treating an AI agent the same way: like a house-sitter you gave the keys to. If the house-sitter is tricked into letting a burglar in, the first question is why they had the keys. The requirement to prove revocation adds a practical twist: taking the keys back counts only if you can show when you did it. Why it matters: On Day 231 we covered websites locking agents out; this is the other route, letting them in and moving the risk to the user. It sets a likely template for banks, brokers and retailers ahead of an agent-driven holiday shopping season. For anyone using a personal agent with payment access, prompt injection is now a personal financial risk, not just a vendor's security problem. Spending limits, confirmation steps and tokenised cards (the ChatGPT Wallet, Day 232; Walmart's gateway, Day 239) matter more as a result. Whether such terms hold up against consumer-protection law is untested.

Market signal

The signal is that confidence in AI still hangs on very few points of trust. One person's return moved Nvidia by nearly a trillion dollars in a day and lifted the whole Nasdaq. One small kernel turned an unreadable 61-gigabyte proof into an accepted result. One proposed number, not yet chosen, is being offered as the industry's brake. And one contract clause decides who pays when an agent is fooled. Expect boards and investors to price key-person risk at AI firms more explicitly, independent verification to become a selling point for AI results, and liability terms for agents to spread from banks to every consumer platform before the rules are tested in court.

Practical takeaways
Name your key people and their backups.

List the individuals and single suppliers your AI plans depend on, and write down what you would do in the first week without each.

Pair AI output with a small independent checker.

Where you can, fix a specification first (tests, a schema, a formal statement) and accept AI work only when a separate tool confirms it meets that specification.

Check the statement, not only the proof.

When a result is called verified, confirm that what was checked is exactly what you asked for, not a close relative.

Ask for the held-out score.

For any benchmark claim, ask which tests the system saw during development and what it scored on the ones it did not.

Ask who measured the safety threshold.

When a vendor cites a capability or safety limit, find out whether the measurement was its own or an outside party's.

Read the agent clause before you connect an agent.

Before giving a personal AI agent access to a bank, broker or shop, read the provider's terms on who is liable for its actions.

Keep proof when you revoke an agent's access.

Save a dated screenshot or confirmation e-mail whenever you remove an agent's permissions, and tell the provider in writing.

Cap what an agent can spend.

Use a tokenised or virtual card with a low limit and require confirmation for payments, so a tricked agent cannot do much damage.

Self-test
What is key-person risk, and how did Nvidia's share price show it on November 23?

It is the danger that a company's value depends on one individual; Nvidia gained about 31%, roughly $950 billion, on the day its founder was reinstated.

What two things did the Lean and Mathlib communities check about the Riemann Hypothesis proof?

That the theorem proved exactly matches Mathlib's agreed statement of the hypothesis, and that Lean's kernel and other independent checkers accept the proof.

What does perplexity measure, and why is it an incomplete limit on AI capability?

It measures how surprised a model is by real text, that is, how well it predicts the next word; tools, planning and reasoning can add capability without lowering it.

Frostbite scored 6.02 times on ten workloads and 3.83 times on fifteen others. What does the gap suggest?

The ten were used during design and the fifteen were held out, so the gap suggests some tuning to the practice set; the held-out figure is the more honest one.

Under Wells Fargo's new terms, who is responsible if a customer's AI agent is tricked by a prompt injection?

The customer, according to press reports of the agreement, until the bank has been told the agent's access is revoked and the customer can prove it.

How does FreeVideo fit a large video model onto an 8 GB graphics card?

A planner keeps only some of the model's blocks in graphics memory at a time, holding the rest in system RAM or streaming them from disk, and uses compressed 8-bit weights.
