Fake Passwords Expose Leaky AI Providers, Congress Reveals 900,000 Approved Chips for China, and Walmart Lets Shopping Agents In Before Black Friday

Dragos's two-million-prompt canary experiment, the House committee's export-licence disclosure, the SEC's Nvidia probe and its insurance gap, Walmart's Agent Gateway for outside AI agents, a growing coalition calling for control of frontier models, and CLI-Anything, the repo turning desktop software into agent tools.

Viral / open-source spotlight

CLI-Anything: turning desktop software into tools an agent can drive

CLI-Anything (github.com/HKUDS/CLI-Anything), from the Data Intelligence Lab at the University of Hong Kong, appeared on November 21 AI trend lists with about 770 new stars, under the slogan 'Making ALL Software Agent-Native'. It is mainly a plugin for coding agents such as Claude Code (installed with /plugin marketplace add HKUDS/CLI-Anything); you point it at a program that has source code and it generates a full command-line interface for it, plus a skill file the agent can read. Ready-made CLIs exist for GIMP, Blender, LibreOffice, 3D-printing files and cloud APIs, and an arXiv paper, 'CLI-Anything: Towards Agent-Native Computer Use', describes the method and a sharing hub called CLI-Hub. Why it is taking off: Yesterday's issue covered computer-use agents that look at the screen and click like a person (Apple's agent, Day 238). That works for any app but is slow, costly and fragile: a moved button breaks it. CLI-Anything takes the other road. A command like 'blender render --scene intro --frames 1-120' is exact, fast, cheap in tokens and easy to check, and agents are already excellent at the terminal. Think of it as giving the agent a remote control with labelled buttons instead of asking it to operate the TV by looking at it. The caveats: it only works where the source code is available, a generated CLI can miss features or get them wrong, and a command line with full access to your files deserves the same permissions caution as any agent. Also trending: minimind (about 850 stars in a day), which trains a 64-million-parameter language model from scratch in about two hours on one GPU, a hands-on way to learn how LLMs are built.

Part 1Two Million Fake Secrets Show Where AI Prompts Leak

Researchers at the industrial-security firm Dragos, led by chief research officer HD Moore, published a study titled 'Three breaches, endless leaks'. From July to November 2026 they sent about two million prompts to widely used AI services. Each prompt contained a unique, trackable fake secret: a URL, a password or a set of AWS keys that led to nothing real. Then they watched for anyone trying to use them. Some did. The team reports signs that data from at least three AI providers was exposed: planted credentials were tried against their test systems and decoy AWS accounts. In one case, secrets sent to two different providers were used together, almost immediately, from a single rented server, which suggests one attacker was inside both. Separately, resellers on an AI inference marketplace could see and keep customer prompts through ordinary logging, without hacking anything, and some prompts sent to US providers ended up with Chinese AI organisations. Some stolen requests were flagged 'zero data retention'. The concept, explained simply: A canary token (or honeytoken) is bait. You plant a fake secret that no honest process would ever use, so any attempt to use it is proof that someone saw it who should not have. It works like dye packs in bank cash: it does not stop the theft, but it tells you it happened and roughly where. The study also shows the 'fourth-party' problem. When you use an AI app, your prompt may pass from the app to a router or marketplace, then to a reseller, then to the company actually running the model. Each hop can log it. A 'zero data retention' flag is a promise about what a provider stores, not a guarantee against an attacker or a careless reseller in the chain. Why it matters: This is the first large, measured look at what happens to prompts after you press Enter, and it follows this month's Fireworks AI breach (Days 228 and 233). Anyone pasting API keys, customer data or internal links into AI tools should assume a leak is possible somewhere along the chain. The cheap fix is to keep secrets out of prompts, use short-lived credentials, and plant your own canaries in places agents can read, so you find out fast.

Part 3An SEC Probe Into Nvidia, and an Insurance Gap

The Wall Street Journal reported on November 17 that the Securities and Exchange Commission has opened a civil investigation into whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors. Neither side has confirmed it, the SEC says it does not comment, and no enforcement action has been announced. Reports link it to Jensen Huang's departure (Day 230) and to Nvidia's decision last year to stop breaking out sales to Singapore, a hub through which chips can reach other countries. A Reuters analysis added that Nvidia's directors and officers are insured for up to $950 million, but the company lacks the insurance that would normally cover its own legal costs and settlements in securities lawsuits. Nvidia paid $5.5 million in 2022 to settle SEC charges over crypto-mining sales. The concept, explained simply: Directors-and-officers (D&O) insurance usually has three parts. 'Side A' pays individual directors when the company cannot. 'Side B' repays the company when it covers its directors. 'Side C', or entity coverage, pays the company's own costs when the company itself is sued over its securities. Some very large firms skip Side C because it is expensive and they can afford to pay out of their own pocket. An investigation does not mean wrongdoing; it is a fact-finding stage that often ends with no action. Why it matters: The world's most valuable chip maker now faces a leadership change, a federal probe and lawsuits without entity insurance, all at once. Disclosure questions, such as where chips really end up, are becoming governance questions for the whole AI supply chain. If your strategy depends on Nvidia supply, nothing changes this week, but a long investigation can distract management in the middle of a product cycle.

Part 4Walmart Opens a Door for Outside Shopping Agents

With Black Friday on November 27, Walmart's Agent Gateway is the clearest example of a big retailer inviting AI agents in rather than blocking them. Announced on October 21 and now in early beta for approved developers, it is an MCP server that lets outside agents, such as ChatGPT, Gemini and Meta's Muse, shop at Walmart and Sam's Club. They do not touch the catalogue or checkout directly: they talk to Sparky, Walmart's own assistant, which does the work. Outside agents never see card numbers or full order histories, customers approve purchases by default, shoppers can set spending limits such as $50 or $100, and Walmart may ask for extra approval even within a limit. Walmart says Sparky users spend about 35-40% more per order. The contrast is Amazon, which blocks outside agents.

The concept, explained simply: This is agent-to-agent commerce. Your agent (the buyer's representative) talks to the store's agent (the seller's representative), much as two lawyers talk for their clients. The store keeps control of prices, stock and payment, and shares only what the task needs. MCP, the Model Context Protocol (see the March MCP deep dive), is the standard plug that lets the two connect. A spending limit plus an approval step is the agent version of a corporate card with a manager sign-off.

Why it matters: Two models of the agent web are forming: open doors with house rules (Walmart, Cloudflare's agent browser on Day 232) and walled gardens (Amazon, sites locking out agents on Day 230). For businesses, it is time to decide which you are. For shoppers, the safest setup this holiday season is a low spending limit and approval for every purchase.

Part 5More Countries Join the Call to Control Frontier AI

The 'Call for Control of Frontier AI Models', launched on September 21 at the UN General Assembly by about 20 countries and the European Commission, keeps gaining signatures. Newsletter roundups this week put the count at 32 states plus the Commission, a figure we could not confirm on official pages, and the President of Finland and the Prime Minister of Norway have issued a joint statement on next steps, saying more countries have joined. The call asks for mandatory testing of frontier models before release and suggests a new international body to set standards, check compliance and convene states when AI capabilities cross agreed thresholds. The United States and China have not signed.

The concept, explained simply: A capability threshold is a pre-agreed line, for example 'a model can meaningfully help build a cyber weapon' or 'a model can improve itself without humans'. Crossing it triggers an agreed response, such as extra testing, restricted release or an international meeting, like an alarm level in a nuclear plant. Pre-release testing means outside evaluators examine a model before the public gets it, rather than after problems appear. Why it matters: Without the two biggest AI powers, the call cannot bind the labs that matter most. But it creates a shared vocabulary and a ready-made structure. If a serious incident happens, governments will reach for exactly this kind of plan, and the EU AI Act's rules for general-purpose models already point the same way.

Market signal

This week's signal is about trust in the plumbing, not new models. Prompts leak through reseller chains, chip licences turn out to be paper rather than shipments, the biggest chip maker faces questions about its own disclosures, and retailers are writing the rules for agents that spend money. The money keeps flowing (record IPO, record leases), but buyers are starting to price the cost of verifying what vendors say. Expect contracts to ask more about subprocessors, audit rights and agent permissions, and expect vendors that can prove where data and chips go to win deals.

Practical takeaways
Keep secrets out of prompts.

Never paste API keys, passwords or private links into an AI tool; use a secrets manager and short-lived tokens that agents fetch when needed.

Plant your own canaries.

Put a few fake credentials in documents and folders your AI agents can read, and set alerts so you know within minutes if one is ever used.

Map your fourth parties.

Ask each AI vendor which routers, resellers and inference providers actually process your prompts, and get it in the contract.

Read export news by shipments, not approvals.

When judging chip supply or China risk, look for shipped and delivered figures; licence approvals overstate what moves.

Check your vendors' governance, not just their products.

For critical AI suppliers, note open investigations, leadership changes and legal exposure as part of vendor risk reviews.

Set spending limits before letting agents shop.

If you try agent shopping this Black Friday, use a low cap, require approval for every order, and use a card you can freeze.

Decide your agent-access policy.

If you run a website or store, choose whether outside agents get an official door with rules or are blocked, rather than leaving it to chance.

Prefer structured tools over screen-clicking.

Where an app has a CLI or API, give agents that instead of computer-use control; it is faster, cheaper and easier to audit.

Self-test
What is a canary token, and what did Dragos use it for?

A fake secret that no honest process would use, so any use proves exposure; Dragos planted them in about two million prompts to see which AI providers' data leaked.

Why does a 'zero data retention' flag not fully protect a prompt?

It is a storage promise by one provider; resellers, routers or an attacker already inside the chain can still see and keep the prompt.

What is the difference between an approved export licence and a shipped chip?

A licence is permission to sell; the chip still has to be shipped and allowed into the buyer's country, and most of the roughly 900,000 approved chips reportedly never moved.

What does 'Side C' D&O insurance cover, and why does it matter for Nvidia?

Entity coverage pays a company's own costs in securities claims; Nvidia reportedly lacks it, so it would pay defence and settlements itself.

How do outside agents shop at Walmart through the Agent Gateway?

They connect over MCP to Walmart's own agent, Sparky, which handles catalogue and checkout; card data stays hidden and customers approve purchases within set limits.

Why is a CLI often better for agents than screen-based computer use?

Commands are exact, fast, cheap in tokens and easy to verify, while clicking on screens is slow and breaks when the layout changes.
