Part 1Anthropic's Second Day: Down About 10%, Five Cents From Its IPO Price

A day after closing its debut at $97.17 (Day 237), Anthropic's stock fell about 10% on November 20 US time, to roughly $87.25 (some outlets quote a close of $88.30, down 9.1%). At its low it came within about five cents of the $83 IPO price. The fall came in a wider sell-off in AI and tech shares. At the same time the underwriters fully exercised their greenshoe option: 147 million extra shares, exactly 15% of the 980 million offered, which takes the deal to 1.127 billion shares and about $93.5 billion in gross proceeds. Add the separate $26 billion private placement and outlets put the total raised at about $119.5 billion. Commentators noted that Facebook and Uber each lost about 11% on their second trading days.

The concept, explained simply: A greenshoe (over-allotment option) lets the banks sell up to 15% more shares than the deal's headline size. They sell the extra shares on day one, which leaves them 'short'. If the price falls below the IPO price, they buy shares back in the market to cover that short, and that buying acts as a cushion that supports the price; this is called price stabilisation. If the price stays above the IPO price, buying back would lose money, so instead they exercise the option and take the extra shares from the company at the IPO price. A full exercise means the banks did not need to buy shares back to support the stock: it had stayed above $83. There is also a 'liquidity drain' effect: a deal this large pulls money out of other stocks, because investors sell what they own to pay for the new shares. Why it matters: Day two shows how quickly first-day excitement can fade, and how a record-sized listing interacts with the rest of the market. For anyone who relies on Anthropic, this is a reminder that its share price will now swing with sector moods unrelated to its products. The real test is the first quarterly report as a public company, not the first week's trading. If ANT ever trades below $83, the banks' stabilisation cushion is already used up, because the greenshoe was exercised.

Part 2An Open-Source AI Attack Kit Knocks EU Staff Systems Offline

On November 19 the European Commission confirmed that attackers used an AI tool in a cyberattack on its internal systems. First signs were detected on October 22, and CERT-EU reportedly identified the tool as ARTEX. Payroll, leave requests, medical reimbursements, timesheets and translation services were disrupted for more than ten days; most systems are back, though one report says payroll failed again after being restored. The Commission says it has no evidence of a broad breach of staff data and knows of no ransom demand, while Politico reported that large amounts of personal data may have been exposed. A week earlier, on November 13, the FBI issued a FLASH alert warning that criminal and state-aligned groups have used ARTEX since at least August against finance, government, technology, defence and healthcare targets, with some intrusions going from first contact to privileged access in under three hours. ARTEX was published on GitHub in July and removed on October 8, but copies and a paid 'ARTEX PRO' edition still circulate.

The concept, explained simply: ARTEX is a multi-agent penetration-testing framework: software that strings AI agents together to do the steps a human hacker would, such as mapping a network, finding weak points, trying exploits and moving to more powerful accounts. It has no model of its own; it plugs into whatever commercial or open model the attacker connects. Defenders talk about 'dwell time' (how long an attacker is inside before being caught) and 'breakout time' (how long from first foothold to spreading further). Agents shrink both, because they work at machine speed and never get tired. The same tools are legitimately used by security teams to test their own systems, which is why they are hard to ban: the difference is permission, not code. Why it matters: This is one of the first confirmed cases of a major government body being disrupted by an AI-driven attack tool, and it fits the trend seen with OpenAI's 'Critical for cyber' rating (Day 236) and the agent-security deals (Days 228 and 237). The lesson for organisations is that monitoring and response must work at the same speed: alerts that a human reviews the next morning are too slow when an intrusion can finish in three hours. Removing a repository from GitHub also did little; once an attack tool is open-sourced, it stays available.

Part 3Apple Is Building an Agent to Run Your Mac, and Raising Walls for Rivals

A job listing first reported by Bloomberg, and covered by TechCrunch on November 19, shows Apple hiring a senior engineering manager to lead work on an 'AI agent that can use a computer'. The listing describes agents that move 'fluidly between tools' and carry out multi-step tasks across apps, browsers, command lines, code and APIs, including writing and fixing their own code. Bloomberg reports Apple has looked at launching as soon as the end of next year, and that the work is early; the listing has since been removed. At the same time, the macOS 27.3 beta adds warning screens asking users to reconsider before giving third-party agents control of their Mac, and enabling ChatGPT's computer-use mode reportedly now takes 14 steps instead of four. ChatGPT, Gemini and Meta's Muse can already operate a Mac this way.

The concept, explained simply: A computer-use agent works a computer the way a person does: it looks at the screen (through screenshots or the accessibility system that screen readers use), decides what to click or type, does it, and checks the result, in a loop. To do this on a Mac, an outside app needs powerful permissions such as Accessibility and Screen Recording, which is exactly where Apple can add friction. The company that owns the operating system is the platform gatekeeper: it decides how easy it is for others to control the device. A built-in agent can be given deeper, safer access (for example through app 'intents' that expose actions directly) while rivals must go through the slower screen-reading route.

Why it matters: The agent race is moving from chat windows to the operating system, and the OS owners (Apple, Microsoft with Copilot, Day 235, and Google) hold a structural advantage. Extra warning screens can be defended as security, since an agent with full control of your Mac is a large risk (see Meta's Muse privacy reports this week), but they also shape competition and may draw regulators' attention, especially in the EU. For users and IT teams, expect a choice between a tightly integrated built-in agent and more capable but harder-to-enable third-party ones.

Part 4Riot Names Anthropic as the Tenant of a $28 Billion Texas AI Campus

Riot Platforms, best known as a bitcoin miner, used its quarterly filing on November 18 to confirm that the 'frontier AI lab' leasing its entire Corsicana, Texas campus is Anthropic. The 15-year lease covers 910 megawatts and is worth about $28 billion in rent over the initial term (about $1.8 billion a year), rising to about $54 billion if both five-year extensions are used. It became binding on November 4. Riot must fund roughly $5.5 billion of development and may reimburse Anthropic up to about $3.6 billion for its data-centre modules; if a phase is more than 270 days late, Anthropic can cancel that phase. Anthropic also leases 191 MW at Riot's Rockdale site, so it takes 1,101 of Riot's 1,151 MW of contracted capacity.

The concept, explained simply: AI data centres are now priced in megawatts of power, not square metres of floor space, because electricity and grid connections are the scarcest input. Bitcoin miners already own sites with big grid connections, cooling and permits ('powered land'), which is why many are turning into AI landlords. In a 'powered shell' deal like this, the landlord provides the building, power and cooling, and the tenant brings and runs the computers. A rough rule: 1 gigawatt can run hundreds of thousands of modern AI chips, so 910 MW is a frontier-scale training and inference site. Delivery deadlines with cancellation rights show who holds power in the deal: the tenant with the money. Why it matters: It shows where part of Anthropic's IPO money is going: long, fixed commitments for power and buildings that will need years of revenue growth to pay for. Concentration cuts both ways: Riot now depends on one customer for almost all its contracted capacity, and Anthropic depends on Riot delivering on time. Combined with Nvidia saying it has stopped shipping licensed chips to China (its guidance now assumes no China data-centre revenue), the picture is of compute being built out fast in the US while the supply map splits along political lines.

Market signal

Anthropic gave back most of its first-day gain within 24 hours even as its banks sold 15% more stock, while the same week brought a $28 billion power-and-buildings commitment in Texas. Nvidia's guidance now assumes zero China data-centre sales, and an open-source attack framework disrupted a major government body. The signal: money for AI capacity is still flowing at record scale, but public investors are pricing in volatility and the security bill is rising alongside the compute bill. Plan for cheaper models, more expensive defence, and vendors whose share prices move with the sector rather than their own news.

Practical takeaways
Do not read a stock's first week as a verdict.

Judge AI vendors on reliability, pricing and quarterly results; first-week swings mostly reflect supply and sector mood.

Check that your detection works in hours, not days.

Test whether an intrusion that reaches admin rights in three hours would trigger an alert someone acts on the same day.

Run authorised AI red-team tests on yourself.

Use agent-based testing tools with written permission, so you find the weak points before an ARTEX-style attacker does.

Protect payroll and HR as critical systems.

The EU attack hit staff pay first; make sure these back-office systems have backups, offline copies and a tested recovery plan.

Audit which agents can control your computers.

List every app with Accessibility, Screen Recording or full-disk access on company Macs and PCs, and remove what is not needed.

Plan for built-in OS agents.

Assume Apple, Microsoft and Google will ship deeply integrated agents; decide your policy for them before they arrive, not after.

Ask your AI providers where their compute comes from.

Long leases with delivery deadlines can slip; ask about capacity plans, regions and what happens if a site is late.

Try a local model for private tasks.

Test a local agent such as Linger on non-sensitive data and compare its quality and speed with your cloud model before trusting it with real accounts. SELF-TEST Q1.
