Microsoft Maps the AI Attack Kit Used by Six Hacking Groups, an Nvidia Rack Surfaces in a Shanghai Loan Filing, and OpenAI Corrects Its Meter

Infrastructure & Economics Governance & Safety

Microsoft's defender guide to ARTEX shows attack agents swapping models and going from a leaked secret to a key vault in five minutes, Bloomberg traces a restricted Blackwell rack through Chinese loan papers, OpenAI's billing correction makes GPT-6.2 limits arrive 40% sooner, Apple removes MiniMax's Talkie worldwide, Adobe counts a 162% rise in AI-referred shopping visits, SAP confirms a security incident, and last30days-skill teaches agents to check what people said this month.

Viral / open-source spotlight

Last30days-skill: an agent skill that researches what people said about a topic in the last 30 days

last30days-skill (github.com/mvanhorn/last30days-skill) is an add-on, called a skill, for coding and chat agents. Give it a topic and it searches recent posts and discussions, ranks what it finds by how much attention each item received (upvotes, likes, views, and the money traded on prediction markets), and has the agent write a short briefing with links to the sources. Without any account or key it reads Reddit with its comments, Hacker News, Polymarket, GitHub and StockTwits. With keys or a logged-in session it adds X, YouTube, Bluesky, TikTok, Instagram, Threads and LinkedIn, and a Brave Search key adds the general web. It can also run with no topic and report what is trending. The repository page showed about 76,400 stars and 6,800 forks when we read it, under the permissive MIT licence, and GitHub's trending page listed it with 675 new stars for the day. It installs as a plugin in Claude Code and, through the shared Agent Skills format, in Codex, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, Gemini CLI and what the page describes as more than 50 other hosts. There are also packages for the Claude desktop and web apps. Why it is taking off: A language model's knowledge stops at its training date, and the questions people most want answered are often about this month: which tool developers have moved to, what users say about a product, what changed after a release. This skill packages the habit of a good researcher, which is to go and look at what people are saying now, and it runs in the agent a developer already uses. The skills format is the other reason. A skill is a folder of instructions and scripts, so one project works across many agents with no rewriting. The caveats are real. Attention is not accuracy: a post can be popular and wrong, and upvotes and prediction markets can be gamed, so the briefing is a map of opinion and not a set of facts. The page says that topics and snippets may be sent to an outside model for ranking unless none is configured. Some sources need paid scraping services or a logged-in browser session, which may conflict with a site's terms of use and is a credential you would be handing to a tool. With the official X interface, recent posts cover about a week, not thirty days. Also trending: Claude-BugHunter, a bundle of security-testing skills, which led GitHub's daily list with 1,608 new stars and about 26,200 in total; minimind (Day 239); and app-store-compliance (github.com/mjmirza/app-store-compliance), a checklist and checker for common App Store and Google Play rejections, with about 7,950 stars.

Part 1Microsoft Publishes a Defender's Guide to ARTEX

On November 25 Microsoft's threat intelligence team published a long technical post on ARTEX, the open-source AI penetration-testing framework behind the attack on the European Commission's staff systems (Day 238). Microsoft names six groups it has seen using it: Storm-0940, Storm-3358, Onyx Sleet, Pearl Sleet, Crystal Typhoon and Salt Typhoon. Press coverage describes three of them as linked to China, two to North Korea and one as a criminal group based in Cambodia. The post says plainly that ARTEX "does not introduce new attack techniques". What it adds is speed. In several Storm-0940 intrusions, privileged access came less than three hours after the first probe. In one case an agent went from searching GitHub for leaked client secrets to reading an Azure Key Vault in five minutes. One password-guessing campaign produced more than 151,000 failed sign-ins and peaked at 2,900 a minute. The post also describes how the software is built. A planner agent reads a shared map of everything found so far (machines, facts, confirmed weaknesses) and writes short instructions that Microsoft calls intents. Worker agents pick up an intent, run ordinary hacking tools from a real command line, and write what they learn back to the map, which wakes the planner again. A reporter agent writes up each confirmed weakness. The operator can connect almost any model, including open-weight models with their safety training stripped out, and a pool feature switches to the next model when one fails, runs out of quota or is rate-limited. The original author removed the code in October, but copies circulate, and Microsoft says a Chinese company, ThreatBook, claimed ownership in November and sells an ARTEX Pro edition. SecurityScorecard is reported to have counted 3,783 servers with an ARTEX control panel reachable from the internet in the 60 days to November 23. The concept, explained simply: Two ideas are worth keeping. The first is the planner-and-worker loop around a shared map. It works like a site manager with a whiteboard: the manager reads the board and hands out small jobs, each worker does one job and writes the result on the board, and the manager plans again from the new board. No single agent has to hold the whole attack in its head, and many workers can run at once. The second is the model pool. Because the framework can swap models mid-task, no one AI company sees the whole attack or can stop it by cutting it off. A model that fails or is blocked is just a reason to try the next. That is why Microsoft tells defenders to look for behaviour and not for names. A signature is a fixed fingerprint, such as the label ARTEX puts on its web requests, and an attacker changes it in a minute. Behaviour is harder to hide: a web server that suddenly starts running command lines, three kinds of secret file read within ten minutes, or one address requesting a hundred different pages a minute. Why it matters: The defences Microsoft lists are old ones, done properly: fewer systems exposed to the internet, no secrets in code, multi-factor sign-in, narrow permissions, and automatic containment that does not wait for a person. The reason they matter more now is the clock. A three-hour or five-minute attack is over before a human analyst has read the first alert, so the response has to be automatic too. Microsoft also reports that decoys worked: fake credentials and fake pages that no real user would touch, so any use of them is a clear alarm. An agent that tries everything is more likely than a careful human to step on one. For anyone building agents, the same design that makes ARTEX effective (a planner, parallel workers, a shared record, a fallback list of models) is the standard design of useful agent systems, which is why safety limits inside one model are not a complete control.

Part 2A Restricted Nvidia Rack Surfaces in a Shanghai Loan Filing

Bloomberg reported on November 27 that Nvidia hardware barred from sale to China is installed at a data centre in Shanghai, according to summaries of the article. The evidence is paperwork. A company called Shanghai Shuoyao pledged a GB300 NVL72 rack, a cabinet holding 72 Blackwell Ultra chips, as security for a loan of 96 million yuan (about $14 million) from Shanghai Innovation Bank, and the rack appears in the bank's filings. One summary adds a second borrower, Shanghai Kezhan, which listed a GB200 server in papers for a 90 million yuan loan. Bloomberg is said to have traced the rack's serial number through a public warranty database to the Taiwanese manufacturer Gigabyte, which declined to name the original buyer. Shuoyao told Bloomberg it is a service provider to Megaspeed, the Singapore company the US Commerce Department added to its Entity List this month (Day 244), and called the relationship ordinary business. Bloomberg said it found no evidence that Megaspeed itself sent the hardware to the site, and Megaspeed denies wrongdoing. The timing is awkward for Nvidia. Jensen Huang is due to testify at a Senate Banking Committee hearing on export controls on December 3, and headlines recalled his earlier remark that a Blackwell rack would not fit in a pocket or a backpack. One outlet reported that Nvidia shares fell 2.6% after the story. The concept, explained simply: Export controls on chips are rules about who may receive them. The Entity List is the US government's list of named organisations that American suppliers may not sell to without a licence. Diversion is what happens when a chip is sold legally to a buyer in one country and then moved on to a place where it could not have been sold. The controls have no tracking device inside the chip, so enforcement depends on records: invoices, shipping papers, warranty registrations and, here, loan documents. A bank that lends against equipment wants the equipment described exactly, because it may have to seize and sell it. That makes a loan filing an honest witness. The borrower had every reason to list the rack accurately and no thought of an export investigator reading it. Matching such public records against each other is called open-source investigation, and it needs no secret access at all.

Why it matters: A full rack of this kind weighs more than a tonne and costs millions of dollars, so this was not a suitcase job. It went through companies, freight and a bank. For regulators, that points to checking the middle of the chain (resellers, service providers and lenders) and not only the first buyer. For Nvidia, the question at the hearing will be how much a seller can be expected to know about where its products end up. For buyers in Southeast Asia, including Thailand, expect more questions before delivery: who owns the data centre, who will use the machines, and who else has access. The shipment halt reported yesterday and this story are two sides of one pressure, and honest buyers will carry part of the cost in paperwork and delay.

Part 3OpenAI Corrects Its Meter and GPT-6.2 Limits Arrive Sooner

On November 26 OpenAI's Thibault Sottiaux said on X that the company had fixed a mistake in how it counted usage of GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol, the two models it released on November 17, according to The Next Web, gHacks, Decrypt and others. Since launch the system had been undercounting what most users consumed. With the count corrected, users reach their plan limits about 40% faster on Astra and 25% faster on Sol than in the week before. OpenAI will not charge for the difference on past use, and paying users on the Go, Plus, Pro and Business plans get a one-time reset of their allowance. Reports say the published prices did not change, and that OpenAI has not said whether it will raise the limits to make up the difference. The coverage is about plan allowances in ChatGPT and OpenAI's own tools; we saw nothing saying that per-token API prices moved. The concept, explained simply: A subscription to an AI product does not sell a number of messages. It sells an allowance of computing work, measured in a unit the customer mostly cannot see. A short question uses a little of it. A long coding task, where the model reads many files and thinks for minutes, uses a lot. The meter is the software that converts each piece of work into units and subtracts them. If the meter runs slow, as an electricity meter might, everyone seems to have a bigger allowance than they really do. The arithmetic of the correction is worth doing once. Reaching the limit 40% faster means each piece of work now counts 1.4 times as much, so the same allowance covers 1 divided by 1.4, or about 71%, of what it did. That is roughly 29% less work, not 40% less. For Sol, 25% faster means 1 divided by 1.25, or 80%: a fifth less.

Why it matters: Anyone who tried the new models in their first week and decided which plan to buy, or how many seats a team needs, made that decision on numbers that were too generous. The first lesson is to re-check after the correction. The second is wider. With a flat-price plan the buyer cannot see the meter and has to trust it, while with pay-per-token API pricing every request reports what it used. A team whose work depends on a coding agent should keep its own record of how much it gets done per day or per week, so that a change like this shows up as a number and not as a feeling that the tool got worse. OpenAI did say what happened and did not bill for the past, which is the right way to handle it, but the episode shows that the real price of a subscription can change without the price list changing.

Part 4Apple Removes MiniMax's Talkie From Its App Store

Apple has removed Talkie, an AI companion app owned by the Chinese company MiniMax, from its App Store in every country, according to Reuters, Caixin and others. Reports differ on whether this happened on November 25 or 26. It followed a joint investigation by ABC News and the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, which alleged that the app's characters sent sexual and abusive messages to users who were children, some as young as ten, and described "degrading language, possessive behaviour and romantic role-play". Apple said it removed the app for sexual content and did not say whether that content reached minors. Talkie was rated 18+ in both app stores, but its age check is reported to have relied on the age a user typed in. The app was still on Google Play when the reports were written, and Google said its store requires appropriate content ratings. One research firm put Talkie at about 11 million monthly users in October. MiniMax has not commented. Talkie and its Chinese sister app Xingye are reported to have produced more than 60% of MiniMax's revenue in 2024. The concept, explained simply: An age rating and an age check are different things. A rating is a label on the shop shelf: it says who the product is meant for. An age check is the person at the door. The weakest kind of check asks the visitor to state their age, which stops only the children who tell the truth. Stronger methods, together called age assurance, include checking an identity document, estimating age from a face, or relying on a signal from the phone's own parental settings. Each costs money, loses some honest customers and raises its own privacy questions, which is why many apps stop at the question. The other idea is the chokepoint. Two companies, Apple and Google, decide what can be installed on almost every phone, so a removal by one of them works faster than most laws. It is also blunt. It does not delete the app from phones that already have it, and it does not touch the website. Why it matters: This is the third companion-chatbot story in a week, after the fight over selling Character.AI's stored conversations (Day 243) and New York's pause on AI chatbot toys (Day 244). The direction is clear: products that let a model play a character with young users are being treated as a child-safety matter first and a technology matter second. For a business, the MiniMax figure is the lesson. A company that earns most of its revenue from one consumer app depends on two gatekeepers it does not control, and one investigation can cut the channel in a day. For anyone building a consumer AI product, the practical point is to decide who the product is for and to check it, and not to rely on a label.

Part 5Holiday Shoppers Ask AI First, Then Buy for Themselves

Adobe Analytics, which measures visits to US retail websites, reported that online spending on Thanksgiving Day reached $7.1 billion, up 10.4% on last year and above its own $6.9 billion forecast, according to press coverage. Visits that arrived from AI services rose 162% from a year earlier, and those visitors were 74% more likely to buy than other visitors, against a 39% advantage a year ago. For Black Friday, Adobe said shoppers had spent $9.7 billion by 6:30 pm Eastern time, up 12.8%, and expected the day to end between $13.0 billion and $13.4 billion. We did not find a final figure. Salesforce's numbers are more modest, pointing to about 4% growth in US online sales for the day. Before the weekend, Salesforce forecast that AI and agents would influence about $75.8 billion of Cyber Week sales, roughly a fifth of the total, while a PwC survey found that only 4% of US shoppers expected to buy gifts through an AI tool. The concept, explained simply: Three different things get called AI shopping, and the numbers above mix them. A referral is a person asking a chat assistant what to buy and then clicking through to the shop to pay in the usual way. Adobe's 162% counts these clicks. Agentic checkout is an agent completing the purchase on the person's behalf, which is what Walmart opened its doors to last week (Day 239). And influence is any sale where AI played some part, a definition loose enough to produce very large totals. The other term is conversion rate: of every hundred visitors, how many buy. Visitors sent by an assistant convert better because they have already done their choosing in the chat. They arrive like a customer who walks in with a shopping list and not like one who is browsing. Why it matters: The honest reading of this weekend is that AI has become a real way for shoppers to find products and is still a small way to pay for them. That matters for where a retailer puts its effort. The immediate job is to be easy for an assistant to read and recommend: accurate product details, prices and stock in a form a machine can use. The second job is to measure this channel separately, because its visitors behave differently. Agent checkout is worth preparing for and is not yet where the money is. A caution on the figures: growth of 162% says nothing about size if the starting point was small, and the reports we saw did not give AI referrals as a share of all visits.

Part 6SAP Confirms a Security Incident After Token Reports

SAP, whose software runs accounting and supply chains at many of the world's largest companies, confirmed a security incident this week. It told BleepingComputer, SecurityWeek and Reuters that it had detected suspicious activity, taken protective measures, and found no evidence of unauthorised access to customers' business data. The details come from German press and SAP has not confirmed them. Der Spiegel reported that attackers stole login tokens belonging to SAP employees and used them to copy and alter source code on GitHub, and that SAP is handling the matter under the name Project Granite. Handelsblatt, citing insiders, said SAP restricted access to GitHub, paused its automated build processes and replaced its code-signing keys. No report we saw says an AI tool was involved. The concept, explained simply: An access token is a long random string that a program presents in place of a password. It is a spare key cut for convenience, so that a developer's tools can reach the code without asking them to log in each time. Whoever holds the string is treated as that developer. A code-signing key is different: it is the company's seal. When SAP ships an update, it signs it, and customers' systems accept the update because the seal is genuine. Replacing signing keys, if that report is right, is what a careful company does when it cannot be sure the seal was out of reach. The risk in an incident like this is called a supply-chain attack: the attacker does not break into a thousand customers, but changes the supplier's code and lets the supplier deliver it.

Why it matters: This is the same weak point as the first story, seen from the victim's side. Microsoft's post describes attack agents searching public code for leaked secrets, and this month has already brought a stolen key that poisoned a coding-agent extension (Day 233) and a coding agent that published its owner's keys (Day 240). Tokens and keys are now the most valuable thing in a software company, and automated attackers look for them first. For SAP customers the advice from security firms is modest and sensible: fix the exact versions of any SAP packages taken from GitHub, look at the release history for versions nobody expected, and follow SAP's advisories. Until SAP says more, treat the press details as unconfirmed.

Market signal

The signal is that the weak points of the AI business are now around the models and not inside them: keys, meters, age checks and paperwork. Attackers with agents go for leaked secrets because that is the fastest way in, so security budgets will move to identity and automatic response, and export controls are being tested through loan filings and serial numbers, so sellers and lenders will ask Asian buyers for more proof. OpenAI's correction shows that the true price of a subscription sits in a meter customers cannot read, and buyers will start asking for usage they can audit. App stores, not legislatures, removed a companion app with millions of users in a day, and the holiday numbers say AI already sends shoppers to retailers and does not yet do much of the paying. One more figure fits yesterday's Bank of England warning: Anthropic's listing is reported to have raised $119.5 billion in total, of which about $26.0 billion came from a private sale of shares to suppliers and partners including AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC. Several of those companies also sell Anthropic its chips and computing, which is what the Bank meant by circular.

Practical takeaways
Detect behaviour, not names.

Set alerts for what an automated attacker does, such as a web server starting command lines or many secret files read in minutes, because tool names and labels are changed easily.

Make containment automatic.

An attack that finishes in minutes cannot wait for a person, so decide in advance which accounts and machines your security tools may isolate on their own.

Plant decoy credentials.

Put fake keys and accounts where no real user would look, and treat any use of them as a confirmed intrusion.

Search your own code for secrets before someone else does.

Turn on secret scanning and push protection in your repositories, and replace any key that has ever appeared in code.

Keep your own record of AI usage.

Track how much work your team gets from a subscription each week, so that a change in the vendor's meter shows up as a number.

Expect to prove who will use your hardware.

If you buy AI servers in Southeast Asia, prepare documents on ownership, end users and access before the supplier or the bank asks.

Check age, do not just label it.

If a product is not meant for children, use a check stronger than asking users to type their age, and know which app-store rules you depend on.

Measure AI referrals as their own channel.

Separate visits that arrive from chat assistants in your analytics, and make product data easy for an assistant to read before investing in agent checkout.

Self-test
In ARTEX, what do the planner and the worker agents each do?

The planner reads a shared map of findings and hands out small tasks; workers carry out one task each with real tools and write the results back to the map.

Why can no single AI company stop an ARTEX attack by cutting off its model?

The framework keeps a pool of models and switches to the next one when a model fails or runs out of quota.

Why is a loan filing good evidence of where a restricted chip ended up?

A lender needs the equipment described exactly so that it can seize it, so the borrower lists it accurately in a public record.

If users reach a limit 40% faster, how much less work does the same allowance cover?

About 29% less, because the allowance now covers 1 divided by 1.4, or roughly 71%, of the earlier amount.

What is the difference between an age rating and age assurance?

A rating is a label saying who the product is for; age assurance is an actual check, such as a document or a signal from the device.

What does Adobe's 162% figure count?

Visits to retail sites that came from AI services, where a person then shops in the usual way; it does not count purchases completed by agents.

What is a code-signing key, and why replace it after an incident?

It is the seal that proves an update comes from the company; if attackers may have reached it, a new one stops them shipping code that customers would trust.
