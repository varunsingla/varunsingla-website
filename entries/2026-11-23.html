A Claimed AI Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, a Coding Agent That Committed the Keys, and the AI Trade's Worst Week

An Anthropic mathematician's Claude-assisted, machine-checked proof claim, Together AI's prompt leak and OpenRouter's purge of three providers, a market correction led by AI stocks, the governors' plan to share AI rule-making with Washington, stalled UN talks on autonomous weapons, and LoopForge, Tencent's workflow engine that puts human approval gates around coding agents.

Models & Frontier Governance & Safety
Viral / open-source spotlight

LoopForge: Tencent's workflow engine that puts approval gates around coding agents

LoopForge (github.com/Tencent/LoopForge) is a self-hosted workspace from Tencent for shipping software with coding agents. It has been appearing on GitHub trending lists this week, with about 5,400 stars according to one tracker and releases up to v0.3.6. It does not replace Claude Code, Codex or Tencent's own CodeBuddy; it orchestrates them. A team writes a standard operating procedure, a fixed workflow that mixes three kinds of step: agent steps (implement, review), deterministic scripts (run the tests, build) and human approval gates, where the run pauses until a person approves it or sends the work back to a chosen step. Work runs in Docker sandboxes against real repositories, results can be delivered as GitHub pull requests, and each run keeps its evidence. A blog reports an MIT licence, which we did not verify. Why it is taking off: Teams have learned that the hard part of coding agents is not getting code written but trusting what comes out. Today's Together AI story is the cautionary tale: an agent with commit rights and no checkpoint. LoopForge turns the informal advice 'keep a human in the loop' into something enforceable, the way a factory line has inspection stations that a part cannot skip. It also lets one vendor's agent write and another's review, a cheap cross-check. It builds on ideas covered earlier: reviewing an agent's plan before it codes (Plannotator, Day 235) and running many agents in parallel (Worktrunk, Day 230). The caveats: it is young software at version 0.3, a self-hosted orchestrator holds powerful repository credentials and must itself be secured, and gates only help if the human at the gate really reads the diff. Also trending: Replit's Missions, which coordinates several agents to plan, build and test toward a stated goal.

Part 1An Anthropic Mathematician Says Claude Helped Prove the Riemann Hypothesis

On November 20 Levent Alpöge, a mathematician on Anthropic's technical staff, posted a four-page note saying he and Claude had proved the Riemann Hypothesis, a 167-year-old conjecture about how prime numbers are spread out and one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, each worth $1 million. He did not publish a readable proof. Instead he released an archive of roughly 175,000 files written in Lean 4, a language in which a computer checks every logical step, and said the AI-written explanation runs to more than 250,000 pages. Reports say MIT's Andrew Sutherland compiled the code independently, and several number theorists told Nature and Science they suspect it will hold up. But no outside expert has confirmed it, Andrew Granville called the write-up garbled, and Scientific American describes rival claims. Alpöge said he held the news for several days after speaking with Dario Amodei; it landed the day after Anthropic's market debut. One widely shared headline saying mathematicians 'confirm' the proof is not supported by its own article.

The concept, explained simply: A proof assistant such as Lean is a very strict referee. You write each step in a formal language and a small, trusted program checks that every step follows from the rules. If the file compiles, the logic is sound. That still leaves two human jobs. First, someone must check that the statement the computer proved really is the Riemann Hypothesis and not a subtly different sentence, like checking that a contract says what both sides think it says before admiring the signatures. Second, mathematicians want to understand why it is true. A proof that is checked but that nobody can read is like a calculator's answer to a sum you cannot follow: probably right, but it teaches you nothing yet. Why it matters: If it survives, this is the first famous open problem settled mainly by an AI system, and it moves the bottleneck in research from finding proofs to understanding them. If it fails, it will fail in a precise, checkable place, which is itself progress over vague claims. Either way, the habit to copy is the one to use with any AI output: ask what exactly was verified, by what, and who has checked that the question answered is the question asked.

Part 2A Coding Agent Committed a Key, and OpenRouter Cut Three

Yesterday's Dragos canary study (Day 239) now has names attached. On November 21 Together AI reportedly disclosed that an AI coding agent working on its public 'xorl' GitHub repository committed a credential. That credential opened a private Hugging Face repository holding stored samples of customer prompts, and an outsider downloaded samples from four days in June and July for three open models. Together's scanner caught the key and reverted the commit, but too late. The intruder then searched the prompts for other credentials and tried them; five were Dragos canaries. Together has not said how many customers were affected. On November 22 OpenRouter said it had off-boarded three inference providers and removed 125 endpoints from zero-data-retention (ZDR) eligibility, stressing that OpenRouter itself was not breached and that many removals were precautionary. It linked to notices from Novita, Phala and Together AI but did not say these are the three it removed.

The concept, explained simply: Secret scanning is a tool that reads code for things that look like passwords or keys. It can run after code is published (detect and alert) or before (push protection, which blocks the upload). The difference is everything on a public repository, where automated bots copy new commits within seconds. Reverting a commit is like snatching back a postcard after it has gone through the sorting office: the copy exists. The only real fix for a leaked key is to revoke it. And a coding agent treats a credential as just another string unless something in its harness stops it.

Why it matters: This is the first major disclosed leak where the careless committer was an AI agent, and it follows the Fireworks AI breach (Day 228) and the poisoned coding-agent extension (Day 233). The lesson for any team using coding agents: block secrets before the push, give agents short-lived and narrowly scoped credentials, and do not keep customer data behind a single long-lived key. For buyers, 'ZDR eligible' is a label a marketplace can remove overnight, so check which providers actually serve your requests.

Part 3The AI Trade Has Its Worst Week in Years

US markets closed on Friday, November 20 in what NPR called a really bad week for the AI boom. Reports say the S&P 500 fell 3.7% on Friday alone and about 8.6% over the week, the Nasdaq lost about 10% and turned negative for 2026, and both the S&P 500 and the Dow entered a correction. The chip index fell 10.7% and Nvidia closed 13.8% lower after falling on nine of ten sessions. Anthropic's newly listed shares ended roughly 23% below their brief $115.10 peak, which implies a price in the high $80s, close to the IPO price discussed on Day 238. Figures vary between outlets. The causes cited: Nvidia's leadership turmoil (Day 230), doubts about data-centre spending, the Federal Reserve's first rate rise in more than three years in September, and political resistance to new data centres after the midterms. The concept, explained simply: A correction is a fall of 10% or more from a recent high; a bear market is 20%. The deeper worry is interconnection, sometimes called circular financing. Chip makers invest in and lend to AI labs, the labs use the money to buy chips and rent cloud capacity, and cloud firms borrow to build the data centres. It is like a row of shops that are each other's best customers and each other's lenders: when one slows down, all the tills go quiet together. Higher interest rates make every borrowed dollar in that loop more expensive. Why it matters: A share-price fall does not change what the models can do on Monday morning. It can change what vendors charge and how long they survive: cash-burning AI suppliers find funding harder, free tiers shrink and discounts end. If your product depends on a small AI vendor, this is the week to check its runway and your exit plan.

Part 4Governors Ask for Federal AI Rules That Leave State Laws

On November 22 Bloomberg reported that the National Governors Association had set out recommendations for splitting AI regulation between Washington and the states. The federal side would handle frontier-model safety: independent safety reviews of advanced models and mandatory reporting of serious security breaches and safety incidents. States would keep authority over consumer protection, discrimination and AI's effect on jobs. That clashes with the White House, which in March asked Congress to override state AI laws that impose 'undue burdens'. It echoes a September letter from 26 state attorneys general. Analysts expect the fight to resume when the new Congress sits in January. The concept, explained simply: Preemption means a federal law overrides state laws on the same subject. The choice is between a ceiling and a floor. A ceiling says: this is the rule everywhere and states may not add to it, which gives companies one rulebook. A floor says: this is the minimum and states may go further, as with the minimum wage. The governors are asking for a federal floor on frontier safety and no ceiling on everyday harms. Why it matters: For anyone shipping AI products in the US, this decides whether you follow one rulebook or fifty. The likeliest outcome on current evidence is layers: federal duties for the biggest model developers, with state laws on hiring, lending and consumer protection continuing to apply to everyone who deploys AI. Build compliance for the strictest state you sell in.

Part 5UN Talks on Autonomous Weapons End With More Talks

The seventh Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons met in Geneva from November 16 to 20, with Switzerland presiding. The central question was whether to start negotiating a binding instrument on lethal autonomous weapon systems. Campaigners reported that Russia blocked the agenda on the first day; a chair's draft proposed 30 negotiating days and a protocol by 2028, while a US paper backed five more years of non-binding work. News summaries say the conference agreed to keep talking without a firm commitment to restrict such weapons. We could not confirm the final text on UN pages. The concept, explained simply: A lethal autonomous weapon selects and attacks a target without a person making that specific decision. The phrase negotiators argue over is 'meaningful human control': a human who understands the situation, can intervene and is accountable. The forum works by consensus, so every state has a veto, like a committee that can act only when nobody objects. That is why some countries want to move the issue to the UN General Assembly, where a majority vote is enough.

Why it matters: Together with the Call for Control (Day 239) and the Trump-Xi split (Day 237), it shows the same pattern: international AI rules are stuck where big powers disagree. In practice, limits on military AI will come from national policy, procurement contracts and the usage terms of the labs themselves, not from a treaty, for some years.

Market signal

The signal this week is a gap between capability and confidence. On the same weekend that an AI system may have produced the most famous proof in mathematics, AI shares had their worst week in years and a coding agent leaked customer prompts. Capability is no longer the question investors or buyers are asking; cost of capital and trust are. Expect funding to tighten for AI firms that depend on borrowed money, inference marketplaces to compete on audited privacy rather than price, and 'verified' (by a proof checker, a secret scanner or a human gate) to become the word that sells.

Practical takeaways
Ask what was verified, and by what.

For any AI result, separate 'a tool checked the steps' from 'a person confirmed it answers the right question'; you need both.

Block secrets before the push.

Turn on push protection and pre-commit secret scanning in every repository an AI coding agent can write to; alerts after the fact are too late on public code.

Revoke, do not just revert.

When a key leaks, rotate it immediately and assume it was copied; removing the commit does not undo the exposure.

Give agents short-lived, narrow credentials.

Use scoped tokens that expire in hours and open only what the task needs, so one leaked key cannot unlock stored customer data.

Pin your inference providers.

If you use a model router, set an allow-list of providers and recheck their data-retention status this week, since eligibility labels just changed for 125 endpoints.

Check your AI vendors' runway.

After a market correction, ask smaller AI suppliers about funding and pricing plans, and keep a tested fallback model or provider.

Build compliance for the strictest state.

With preemption unsettled, design US AI products to meet the toughest state rules on hiring, lending and consumer protection.

Put a human gate before anything ships.

Make agent-written code pass tests, a second reviewer and a named person's approval before merge, and make the gate impossible to skip.

Self-test
What does it mean that the claimed Riemann proof is 'checked in Lean', and what does that not settle?

A computer has confirmed every logical step follows the rules; it does not settle whether the formal statement truly is the Riemann Hypothesis or give humans an understanding of why it is true.

Why did reverting the commit not protect Together AI's customers?

On a public repository a leaked key can be copied within seconds, so the credential was used to download prompt samples before the revert; only revoking the key ends the risk.

What is the difference between a correction and a bear market?

A correction is a fall of 10% or more from a recent high; a bear market is a fall of 20% or more.

What is circular financing in the AI sector?

Chip makers and cloud firms invest in or lend to AI labs that then spend the money on their chips and capacity, so trouble at one company spreads to the others.

In preemption terms, what is the difference between a floor and a ceiling?

A floor sets a federal minimum that states may exceed; a ceiling sets one national rule and bars states from adding their own.

What does a human approval gate do in a LoopForge workflow?

It pauses the run at a set step until a person approves it or sends the work back to an earlier step.
