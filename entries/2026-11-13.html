Nvidia Loses Its Founder-CEO, Anthropic's IPO Book Fills on Day One, and Big Websites Lock Out AI Agents

Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia's CEO with CFO Colette Kress stepping in as interim leader, Anthropic's $70-$78 IPO is reported covered on its first roadshow day with a 20% retail slice, and airlines, retailers and banks push back on third-party AI agents acting for customers.

Viral / open-source spotlight

Worktrunk, git worktrees for running many coding agents in parallel

Worktrunk (max-sixty/worktrunk, by Maximilian Roos, known for PRQL and Xarray) is an open-source Rust command-line tool, invoked as wt, that manages git worktrees for parallel AI coding agents. A worktree is a second (or tenth) checkout of the same repository in its own folder, each on its own branch, so several agents can edit code at once without overwriting each other's files. One command, for example wt switch -c -x claude feat, creates the branch, the folder and launches the agent inside it; wt list shows every worktree with its status, and wt remove cleans up. It also adds hooks for local automation and per-branch CI status and summaries. Install with Homebrew or cargo install worktrunk. Why it is taking off: GitHub's weekly trending page lists it at about 2,300 new stars this week, alongside claude-mem (cross-session agent memory, about 7,500 stars this week) and addyosmani/agent-skills. Developers have moved from one assistant in one editor to several agents working at once, and plain git worktrees are fiddly (the README notes you type the branch name three times). Worktrunk turns parallel agents from a juggling act into one command. Caution: star counts are from snapshots and the 'most popular worktree manager' claim is the project's own; parallel agents still need human review before branches are merged.

Part 1Nvidia's Jensen Huang Resigns; CFO Colette Kress Is Interim CEO

Nvidia's board accepted Jensen Huang's resignation as president, chief executive and director on November 11, effective immediately, according to an 8-K filed with the SEC; the company announced it on November 12. CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO while keeping the finance role, and Harvey Jones becomes Nvidia's first-ever independent board chair. The board says it will search internal and external candidates for a permanent CEO. The departure followed days of reporting about Huang's personal conduct that coverage describes as raising national-security questions; those details are still developing and are not set out in the filing. Shares fell more than 13% in early trading on Thursday before recovering part of the drop.

The concept, explained simply: Key-person risk is what happens when one person is so tied to a company's strategy, relationships and reputation that losing them suddenly moves the share price. Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and ran it for over three decades. Splitting the jobs of chair (who runs the board that supervises management) and CEO (who runs the company) is the textbook protection: the board can act independently when the boss is the problem. Why it matters: Nvidia supplies most of the world's AI training chips, so its leadership is now an AI-industry risk, not just a company story. Kress is well known to investors, which helps continuity, but chip roadmaps (Rubin, Vera), China export talks and giant customer deals were often negotiated founder-to-founder. Expect customers and analysts to watch whether delivery schedules, pricing and partnerships stay on track. For any organisation, the lesson is to name successors and separate oversight from execution before a crisis.

Part 2Anthropic's IPO Order Book Is Reported Covered on Day One

Anthropic launched its IPO roadshow on November 10, offering 825 million Class A shares at $70-$78 each for a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT (covered here yesterday). CNBC, citing people familiar with the matter, reported the book was already covered at that range on the first day, and Bloomberg reported orders covering the shares on offer; one market note describes demand as several times oversubscribed. Anthropic is reportedly reserving about 20% of the deal for retail investors, far above the usual single-digit share. At the range the raise would be about $58-64 billion; pricing is expected around November 18-19 with trading to follow.

The concept, explained simply: Book-building is how a big IPO finds its price. Banks spend the roadshow collecting orders from investors: 'I'll take X shares at up to $Y.' If orders exceed the shares on offer, the book is 'covered'; if they exceed it several times, it is 'oversubscribed', and the company can raise the price or the range. A retail allocation is the slice reserved for ordinary investors through brokerage apps instead of large funds.

Part 3Airlines, Retailers and Banks Push Back on Third-Party AI Agents

A late-October roundup and follow-up reports say users of consumer agents such as Meta's Muse, xAI's Grok and OpenAI's Dots and Instinct have hit blocks on major sites. Delta says it has no agreements allowing outside agents to book flights and wants to protect customers from unauthorised automated activity; Amazon and Yelp are named as blocking; JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab say agents cannot touch customer accounts. United has pointed to terms of use that ban bots and unauthorised automated processes, though a specific block was not confirmed. Travel analysts note automated search and price-monitoring traffic generates heavy load without bookings. The concept, explained simply: When an AI agent visits a website for you, the site sees a robot, not a person. Websites already use bot-management tools to stop scrapers and scalpers, and those same tools now catch helpful agents too. The missing piece is agent identity and authorisation: a way for the agent to prove 'I act for this verified customer, with these limits', like a signed letter of authority, so the site can say yes to good agents and no to bad bots.

Why it matters: Agentic commerce only works if the shops let the agents in. This is the next stage of the robots.txt opt-out story covered last week: first sites decided whether AI could read them, now they are deciding whether AI can act on them. Expect a split between 'walled garden' companies that only accept their own or partnered agents, and open protocols for signed agent identity. If you are building agents, plan for official APIs and partnerships rather than screen-scraping your way through someone else's checkout.

Market signal

The AI trade is learning to price people and plumbing, not just models. Nvidia's 13% early drop on a leadership change shows how concentrated AI infrastructure risk is in one supplier, while Anthropic's covered book on the same day shows investor appetite for frontier labs is intact. Underneath, financing is getting more creative: Naver's AI Factory with NVIDIA and Brookfield is funded through a Brookfield special-purpose vehicle that owns the GPUs and charges Naver by use (about $9 billion), with NVIDIA's $1 billion equity stake conditional on that financing. Practical takeaways
Map your key-person risks.

List the vendors and internal teams whose plans depend on one leader, and note what happens if that person leaves tomorrow.

Separate oversight from execution.

Make sure whoever approves an AI programme is not the same person who runs it, so problems can be escalated.

Diversify critical AI suppliers.

Where a single chip, cloud or model vendor is a hard dependency, keep a tested fallback route.

Read the book, not the hype.

Treat 'oversubscribed' as a demand signal, and judge an IPO by the final price, lock-ups and disclosed costs.

Build agents on permission, not scraping.

Use official APIs or partnerships for bookings and payments; assume websites will block unauthenticated agents.

Give each coding agent its own worktree.
