2) CLAUDE HAIKU 5.5 AND THE ECONOMICS OF SMALL MODELS

Reports say Anthropic released Claude Haiku 5.5 on October 7. Listed prices are $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens for prompts up to 100K tokens, versus $1 and $5 for Haiku 4.5 (about 75% cheaper on average, per Anthropic). Prompts longer than 100K tokens cost more ($0.50 and $2.50). Vendor-reported benchmarks: 72.4% on the offline subset of OSWorld 2.1 (a computer-use test) versus 15.7% for Haiku 4.5; an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index score of 43 at max effort. A reviewer notes an over-refusal bug depresses one automation benchmark (35%) and cost-per-task numbers are provisional. The concept, explained simply: 'effort controls' and tiered pricing. You pay per token, but the model can also be told how hard to think (Low to Max). One writeup reports Humanity's Last Exam scores rising from about 31% at Low effort (a fraction of a cent per task) to about 46% at Max (about 21 cents). So the real cost of a task is price per token times tokens used, and thinking effort is a dial you set per job. Why it matters: the headline '75% cheaper' is only true for your workload if prompts stay under the 100K tier and the new tokenizer (which can count more tokens for the same text) does not inflate your input. Always measure cost per completed task on your own data. Cheap models that can operate a computer also raise the stakes of yesterday's lesson on action boundaries: a fast, cheap agent can make a mistake many times over.

3) THE COMPUTE RACE: US ~56GW, CHINA ~24GW

A SemiAnalysis estimate, as relayed by Financial Times coverage in an aggregator, puts China's operational AI compute at about 24 gigawatts, closing in on the US total of about 56GW. Separately, a bipartisan US energy bill is reported that would fast-track permits for AI data centers. These are secondhand figures and definitions of 'AI compute' vary.

The concept, explained simply: compute measured in power. Chips differ by generation, so analysts count the electricity a data center can draw as a rough, comparable unit of capacity. Power, not chips alone, is increasingly the bottleneck: grid connections, permits and cooling decide how fast capacity comes online. Why it matters: where cheap, abundant power exists, AI capacity gets built, and policy fights over permits and export controls are now about electricity as much as silicon.

4) SAFETY AND JOBS SIGNALS

Common Sense Media's Youth AI Safety Institute reportedly rated ChatGPT for Teens an 'Unacceptable Risk' after more than 4,000 test prompts. A McKinsey report is said to estimate that 11 million Americans will need to change occupations by 2035. Both come from a single aggregator roundup and I have not verified them against the primary reports. Also reported: an Apple event on October 13 for a smart-home hub (not AI-specific).

Market signal

Two signals: the price floor for capable models keeps falling (Haiku 5.5 at $0.10/$0.50) while interfaces get richer (GPT-6 Intelligent UI), so differentiation is moving from raw model access to experience and reliability; and compute is now discussed in gigawatts, tying AI directly to energy policy. Caveat: most figures today are vendor-reported or secondhand.

Practical takeaways
Measure cost per task, not per token.

Count tokens used, retries and the effort setting; a cheaper model is not always a cheaper job.

Check pricing tiers.

Look for context-length thresholds (like 100K) and tokenizer changes before trusting a '75% cheaper' claim.

Match effort to the job.

Use Low effort for routing and extraction, Max only where accuracy pays for itself.

Treat generated UI as untrusted.

Render model-made forms and buttons through a fixed component set and validate submitted data.

Verify vendor benchmarks.

Look for independent scores and known bugs (such as over-refusal) before switching models.
